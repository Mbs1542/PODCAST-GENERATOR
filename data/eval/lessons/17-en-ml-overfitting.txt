In modern machine learning, especially deep learning, regularization takes really creative, algorithmic forms. Take neural networks, for example. In deep nets, you have millions, sometimes billions of parameters, so classical L2 regularization—what we call weight decay in deep learning—it helps, sure, but it's often not enough on its own. One of the most famous techniques we use instead, or rather in addition to weight decay, is called Dropout. And the intuition here is so fun. Think about an athletic team, like a basketball team or soccer team, right? If you have one star player, like LeBron, the rest of the team gets lazy. They basically just pass the ball to LeBron every single possession, and they never learn to shoot, they never learn to create plays under pressure. And then, what happens if LeBron gets injured or the other team double-teams him? The whole offensive system completely collapses. So imagine during practice, the coach says, okay, every twenty minutes, I am randomly benching half the players on the court. Completely at random. What happens then? The remaining players are forced to step up. They can't rely on the star player to bail them out. They have to learn robust, generalizable skills. That is literally what Dropout does to a neural network during training. At every training step, you set the activation of a randomly chosen subset of neurons to zero, with some probability p, usually like 0.5 for hidden layers. The network can't rely on any single neuron or specific co-adaptation of features. It has to learn redundant, distributed representations. And then at test time, you turn Dropout off, but you scale the weights down by 1 minus p, or you scale during training with inverted dropout, so the expected output matches. Another beautifully simple technique in iterative algorithms is early stopping. You just track your validation loss during gradient descent, and as soon as that validation loss stops decreasing and starts creeping back up—even while your training error is still plunging down to zero—you stop training. You literally pull the plug. It's regularization by limiting the capacity via optimization time. You don't let the model explore the crazy, jagged corners of the parameter space. That's how you guarantee you haven't cheated yourself. Now, to wrap up today, I want to give you a very practical diagnostic rubric. When you're sitting at your desk, debugging a model that isn't performing well, how do you know what to do next? It always comes back to diagnosing whether you have high bias or high variance. And the way you diagnose this is by comparing three numbers: your human benchmark or Bayes optimal error, your training error, and your validation error. Scenario one: your training error is 15%, your validation error is 16%, but human-level performance or what you know is achievable on this task is 1%. What's your problem? High bias, underfitting. The model can't even fit the training data properly. Regularization will not help you here! In fact, increasing lambda or adding dropout will make it worse. If you have high bias, you need to add capacity: make the network deeper, use a more complex model, engineer better features, decrease lambda. Scenario two: your training error is 1%, amazing, but your validation error is 12%. Huge gap between training and validation. That's high variance, classic overfitting. The model memorized the noise in the training set and failed to generalize. This is where today's entire lecture comes into play. What do you do? Get more data if you can, because more data shrinks variance. If you can't get more data, you regularize: add an L2 penalty, try Lasso if you suspect sparsity, add dropout, use early stopping, or simplify the model architecture.
